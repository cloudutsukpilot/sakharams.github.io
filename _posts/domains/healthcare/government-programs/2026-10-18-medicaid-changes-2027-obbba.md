Millions of people could lose Medicaid coverage over the next few years. Not because they stopped qualifying, but because of new paperwork: monthly work hours to report, eligibility checks twice a year, and less room to fix mistakes after the fact. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed on July 4, 2025, made the biggest changes to Medicaid since the Affordable Care Act, and most of them take effect between late 2026 and 2028.

This post covers what changes, who's affected, when each change starts, and what it means for the systems that run Medicaid.

> Dates here come from the law as summarised by the National Association of Medicaid Directors. States implement these rules on their own timelines within federal deadlines, and some can get extensions, so always check your state's guidance.

## The timeline at a glance

| Change | Who it applies to | Effective |
|---|---|---|
| One-year funding block for certain family planning providers | Large non-profit reproductive health providers | July 4, 2025 (challenged in court) |
| Work and community engagement requirements | Expansion adults aged 19–64 | December 31, 2026 (extensions possible to the end of 2028) |
| Eligibility checks every six months | Expansion adults | December 31, 2026 |
| Shorter retroactive coverage | All new applicants | Applications from January 1, 2027 |
| Cost sharing up to $35 per service | Expansion adults above 100% of the federal poverty level | October 1, 2028 |
| Provider tax phase-down | Expansion states | Starting in fiscal year 2028 |
| $50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program | States, for rural providers | Fiscal years 2026–2030 |

"Expansion adults" means the adults who became eligible under the Affordable Care Act's Medicaid expansion: working-age adults with incomes up to 138% of the federal poverty level, in states that expanded.

## Work requirements: 80 hours a month

To keep coverage, expansion adults aged 19 to 64 must show **80 hours a month** of qualifying activity:

- paid work;
- community service or volunteering;
- a work programme or job training;
- school, at least half time;
- or a mix of these that adds up to 80 hours.

Some people are **exempt**, including parents and caretakers of children aged 13 and under, people with disabilities or serious medical conditions, pregnant and postpartum people, and some others such as people in substance-use treatment. States must verify compliance, using data they already hold wherever possible, and can check before someone enrols as well as during coverage.

The federal deadline is December 31, 2026, though states can start earlier. A state that shows it's making a good-faith effort can get an extension up to the end of 2028.

The flexibility is real: volunteering and school both count. The risk is mostly administrative. People who qualify but don't report their hours, or whose reports don't match state data, can still lose coverage.

## Eligibility checks every six months

Until now, most Medicaid members renewed once a year. From **December 31, 2026**, states must redetermine eligibility for expansion adults **every six months**. Everyone else stays on yearly renewals.

More renewals mean more chances to drop off: a changed job, a raise that crosses the income line, a missed letter. Expect more churn, where people lose coverage and then re-enrol a few months later.

## Less retroactive coverage

Retroactive coverage lets Medicaid pay for care from before someone applied, as long as they were eligible at the time. It exists so that people who apply after a hospital visit aren't left with the bill.

For applications submitted **from January 1, 2027**, retroactive coverage shrinks from up to three months to:

- **one month** for expansion adults;
- **two months** for everyone else.

Some states had already shortened or waived retroactive coverage under waivers. For hospitals, this means less chance to get paid for uninsured patients who later enrol.

## New copays

From **October 1, 2028**, states must charge cost sharing to expansion adults with incomes above **100% of the federal poverty level**:

- up to **$35 per service**;
- total cost sharing capped at **5% of family income**;
- some services exempt, including primary care, behavioral health, and care at community health centres and rural health clinics.

## Provider taxes and rural hospitals

States raise part of their Medicaid funding through taxes on providers, and those taxes help pay for higher Medicaid rates. The law freezes existing provider taxes and, for expansion states, phases the main threshold down from 6% toward **3.5%**, starting in fiscal year 2028.

Rural hospitals rely heavily on Medicaid, so less state money could mean lower payment rates for them. To soften that, the law created a **$50 billion Rural Health Transformation Program** for fiscal years 2026 to 2030. States apply for grants and must use them on approved activities. It's a safety net, but analysts don't expect it to fully offset the cuts, and not every provider will benefit.

## Family planning providers

The law blocks Medicaid funding for one year, from July 4, 2025, to certain non-profit providers that mainly deliver reproductive health services and offer abortions, which in practice means Planned Parenthood. The provision was challenged in court within days of signing. Check the current status before relying on it.

## What it means for systems

For anyone building or running Medicaid eligibility, enrollment and claims systems, this is a big change.

- **Eligibility engines** need new rules: work-hour verification, exemption tracking, six-month renewal cycles, and different retroactive windows by population.
- **Data integrations** grow: payroll and wage data, school enrollment, and other state data to verify activity without asking members for paperwork.
- **Member notices** have to be clearer and more frequent, or coverage loss will be driven by confusion.
- **Providers** should check eligibility at every visit. With six-month renewals, coverage can lapse between appointments.
- **Claims and cost sharing:** from October 2028, claims for expansion adults will carry new copays, with exemptions by service type.

## Conclusion

OBBBA keeps Medicaid's structure but changes who stays on it and how often they're checked. Work requirements and six-month renewals start at the end of 2026, shorter retroactive coverage in 2027, and new copays in late 2028. The biggest risk is losing coverage over paperwork rather than eligibility.
